\section{Motivation}

% 1. Imaging and comparison
%  - Volumetric imaging (CT, MRI, 3D US) is central to diagnosis, treatment
%    planning, image-guided intervention and monitoring.
%  - Much of its value lies in comparison: across time points, against a
%    reference or atlas, across modalities.
Radiological imaging is central to modern medicine. Volumetric modalities such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and three-dimensional ultrasound (US) allow clinicians to look inside the body non-invasively, and they underpin diagnosis, treatment planning, image-guided intervention, and the monitoring of disease over time. Rarely, however, is a single image examined in isolation: much of the clinical value of imaging lies in comparison of one acquisition against another, of a patient against a reference or of multiple modalities.

% 2. Registration
%  - Definition: estimate a spatial transformation that brings images into a
%    common frame so that corresponding anatomy coincides.
%  - Uses: motion (respiratory, cardiac), multimodal fusion (diagnosis,
%    intra-operative guidance), longitudinal follow-up (tumour progression),
%    atlas-based propagation of annotations.
%  - Usual formulation: maximise a measure of image similarity, subject to a
%    regularity constraint on the transformation.
% REVIEW: "facilitating comparison and translations across different images": "translations" reads as the geometric transformation here; use "transfer of information".

Bringing images into such comparison is the task of \emph{image registration}: the estimation of a spatial transformation that places two or more images in a common coordinate frame, so that corresponding anatomical locations coincide, facilitating comparison and translations across different images. It allows quantifying anatomical motion between time points, such as respiratory or cardiac deformation, it fuses complementary modalities for diagnosis and intra-operative guidance, it follows disease or tumour progression across longitudinal scans and it propagates expert annotations from an atlas onto new subjects for automatic segmentation. In each of these scenarious the goal is to relate one anatomy to another by maximisation of some metric of image similarity while constraining the transformation to be anatomically plausible.

% 3. The problem: registration depends on the scenario
%  - Anatomy, modality, protocol, application and patient all change what
%    "similar" means and which deformations are plausible.
%  - Appearance: lung tissue denser at exhale than inhale; ultrasound
%    reflects and attenuates where X-rays pass; MR contrasts differ.
%  - Deformation: large displacements (posture, breathing), sliding organs,
%    tissue removed between scans (resection, cf. BraTS-Reg), partial fields
%    of view (freehand US sweeps).
%  - Clinicians know these characteristics: which structure is which and
%    how it moves. The algorithm only sees grey values. This knowledge gap is
%    the problem the thesis addresses.
However, image registration is heavily tied to the scenario it is applied to. These include, but are not limited to, anatomical structures of interest, imaging modalities, acquisition protocols, clinical applications, or patient characteristics. The diversity directly influences the definition of similarity and the plausibility of deformations. Appearance changes can derive from variations in imaging conditions. Breathing-related density changes of lung tissue will result in inconsistent grey values, the appearance of vessels in MRI will change by the infusion of contrast agents and different acquisition physics rely on different contrast mechanisms for image composition. The deformations and the associated plausibility are scenario-dependent as well, as large displacements can occur due to posture or breathing. Finally, the images may not even show the same content, for instance due to differing fields of view or resected tissue. In contrast to image registration algorithms, clinicians can compensate and adjust for these differences implicitly by connecting their semantic knowledge of anatomy and acquisition for assessment and comparison of multiple images. This thesis investigates how to bridge this knowledge gap by introducing semantic knowledge into the registration process in the form of anatomical shape.

Anatomical shape, in the form of segmentations, surfaces, point clouds, or statistical shape models, provides exactly this kind of knowledge. If a structure is delineated in both images, the change in position and deformation can be relayed to the registration algorithm as additional information. Shape thus acts as a semantic anchor that addresses the difficulties mentioned above. A segmented structure retains its identity when its grey values change, while corresponding structures provide direct correspondences that remain under large displacements. Furthermore, combined with population-based information, such as statistical shape or deformation models, shapes can help to assess the plausibility of transformation. These advantages are within reach today, as segmentation frameworks such as nnU-Net and TotalSegmentator, and self-supervised foundation models that adapt to new structures with few annotations, make anatomical segmentations widely available at low cost \cite{isensee2021nnu,wasserthal2022totalsegmentator,xu2025generalizable}.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

Using shape as an informant in image registration requires two things: a way to encode anatomical shape into representations that a method can compare and manipulate, and a way to use those representations to determine the deformation.


\paragraph{Encoding shape.} The first part asks \emph{how the shape of anatomy can be encoded into compact, comparable, and robust representations}. Here, this thesis investigates
\begin{itemize}
    \item how anatomical shape can be captured by learned encodings in a common latent space suitable for registration (\chapref{chap:MIUA24});
    \item how statistical shape models can be built on implicit neural representations so that they remain robust to noisy, sparse, or partial observations (\chapref{chap:BVM25}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Shape-informed registration.} The second part asks \emph{how such shape representations, and continuous representations of anatomy more broadly, can inform and improve registration}. Here, this thesis investigates
\begin{itemize}
    \item how implicit neural representations can serve as sharp, continuous atlases that jointly optimise a shared representation and the deformations relating individuals to it (\chapref{chap:WBIR24});
    \item how a registration method that aligns semantic label features can be made self-configuring, so that it applies without manual tuning across the wide range of anatomies and modalities encountered in practice (\chapref{chap:TMI});
    \item how a reconstruction problem, assembling a three-dimensional volume from freehand ultrasound, can be solved as the registration of point clouds against a geometric reference (\chapref{chap:IJCARS}).
\end{itemize}

Combined, the two parts trace an arc from representing shape to registering with it, and support the thesis that anatomical shape is a cheap and effective way to bring semantic knowledge into registration.

\paragraph{Recurring considerations.} Two questions are encountered thoughout both parts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{How anatomy is represented.} Each chapter describes makes use of different techniques to descirbe anatomy: learned shape encodings (\chapref{chap:MIUA24}), an implicit neural shape model (\chapref{chap:BVM25}), a continuous neural field (\chapref{chap:WBIR24}), semantic label features (\chapref{chap:TMI}), and point clouds (\chapref{chap:IJCARS}). Setting these choices against one another is what the representation taxonomy of \secref{sec:bg-shapes} is for.
    \item \emph{Where learning is used.} The chapters place learning at different points: encodings trained to transfer across anatomical structures (\chapref{chap:MIUA24}); a framework that restricts learning to feature extraction, solves for the deformation by optimisation, and configures itself for each task (\chapref{chap:TMI}); and representations optimised per instance at test time rather than predicted by a trained network (\chapref{chap:BVM25}, \chapref{chap:WBIR24}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Organisation and Contributions}
\label{sec:organization}

The thesis consists of a background chapter, five method chapters, and a concluding summary. \chapref{chap:background} surveys the shape representations used throughout the thesis, implicit neural representations, and the fundamentals of medical image registration. The five method chapters (\chapref{chap:MIUA24}--\chapref{chap:IJCARS}) present the methodological contributions: Each is self-contained, comprising its own introduction, methodology, experiments, and discussion. Finally, \chapref{chap:summary} draws the contributions together and discusses them across chapters.

\paragraph{Part~I: Encoding shape.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \chapref{chap:MIUA24} introduces learned shape encodings that embed segmented anatomy into a common latent space and applies them to generalised three-dimensional registration across differing structures. The ideas and methods were published in:

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \noindent\fullcite{grossbrohmer2023generalised}

    \item \chapref{chap:BVM25} develops a statistical shape model built on implicit neural representations, yielding robust modelling of anatomical variation from imperfect observations. The ideas and methods were published in:

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \noindent\fullcite{grossbrohmer2025robust}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Part~II: Shape-informed registration.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \chapref{chap:WBIR24} presents \mbox{SINA}, sharp implicit neural atlases obtained by jointly optimising a continuous representation and the deformations that relate individual images to it. The ideas and methods were published in:

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \noindent\fullcite{grossbrohmer2024sina}

    \item \chapref{chap:TMI} presents convexAdam, a self-configuring, dual-optimisation registration method that attains state-of-the-art results across a wide range of tasks without manual tuning. The ideas and methods were published in the following shared-first-author work:

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \noindent\fullcite{siebert2024convexadam}

    \item \chapref{chap:IJCARS} recasts three-dimensional freehand ultrasound reconstruction as reference-based point cloud registration, using a geometric reference in place of image intensities. The ideas and methods were published in:

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \noindent\fullcite{grossbrohmer20253d}
\end{itemize}
